Beasts of Second-Hand Knowledge

October 24, 2026 — medieval animals drawn from hearsay, not eyesight: the trumpet-trunked elephant, the bonnacon, the goose that grows on trees, and the whale that pretends to be an island. ← back to blog

Most medieval artists drew animals they had never seen. Not because they were clumsy — the same hands produced delicate portraits of hares, owls, and songbirds they could watch in the fields — but because for elephants, lions, tigers, whales, and whole menageries of real beasts, there was simply nothing to look at. Travel beyond your village was rare; the exotic was a rumour, passed through one manuscript, then another, then another, each scribe copying the words but never the animal.

What they drew instead was memory of a description of a rumour. The results are some of the most charming failures in art history — creatures that are recognisably *something*, but assembled from a vocabulary of parts that never coexisted in nature. This page is a visual tour of that tradition, and of the specific manuscripts that preserve it.

01The elephant with a trumpet for a trunk

The hearsay
A beast so vast that no tree can hold it up when it leans on one; it has no knee-joints, so it sleeps leaning against a tree; if it wants to lie down, another elephant must come and prop it up. And its trunk is a kind of trumpet. Bestiary tradition, after Pliny the Elder

The elephant was the superstar error of medieval art. The trunk was usually drawn as a trumpet — a straight or bell-shaped tube — because that's what the word 'trunk' suggested to someone who'd never seen an elephant use one. In Matthew Paris's famous drawing, done from an actual elephant brought to the Tower of London in 1255, you can see the struggle: he was drawing a real animal, but he had no visual grammar for it, so the result is a horse-sized creature wearing a trunk like a coronet. It is the single most-discussed drawing in medieval animal art, precisely because it is the rare case where an artist had the real thing in front of him and still didn't know how to draw it.

02The lion that looks nothing like one

The hearsay
The lion has three natures: it erases its tracks with its tail, it sleeps with its eyes open, and its cubs are born dead and stay dead for three days until the father wakes them by roaring over them. Bestiary tradition, after Isidore of Seville

You would think the lion — the symbol of Christ, of kings, of the Evangelist Mark — would be the best-drawn animal in Europe. It is not. Because the lion was the most allegorically important animal, artists drew from the *text* rather than the beast: a mane like a sunburst, a face like a human grinning, paws like hands. In illuminated manuscripts the lion often looks more like a large curly dog than a predator. The more sacred the animal, the more it was drawn from doctrine — and the doctrine said the lion sleeps with open eyes and roars cubs back to life, not that it has a particular jaw shape.

03The crocodile, drawn as a two-legged worm

The hearsay
A river beast of Ethiopia, some 40 cubits long, with four legs like a serpent's, armored scales, and the habit of weeping after it eats a man. Bestiary tradition, after Isidore of Seville

The crocodile was the ultimate rumour-beast: something enormous that lives in a river, has scales like a fish but legs like a land animal, and eats people. The description is a tangle of contradictions, so medieval artists resolved it by drawing a giant serpent with legs bolted on. This one even has bird feet. But the most interesting part of this image is not the crocodile — it's the small animal crawling into its mouth. That's the hydrus, a legendary snake from Pliny who rolls itself in mud, lets the crocodile swallow it, and then eats its way out. The medieval mind loved this story because it was a perfect allegory of Christ descending into hell to defeat the devil from within.

04The whale that is also an island

The hearsay
A fish so vast that sailors mistake its back for an island; they land on it, light a fire to cook their meal, and the whale, feeling the heat, dives, drowning them all. Bestiary tradition, after the Physiologus

The aspidochelone is drawn like a fish wearing a landscape. This is a rumour that has *consequences* — it warns sailors not to light fires on strange islands — so the illustration is a story, not a portrait. You can see the fire they've lit on its back, and the whale's eye looking up, worried. It's one of the few medieval animals where the artist had to draw a sequence of events, not just a shape, which is why it works so well as a picture even when the whale itself is anatomically a cod.

05The unicorn, hunted like a maiden

The hearsay
A small animal like a kid goat, with a single horn; so fierce that no hunter can catch it — but a young girl who sits alone in the forest will draw it, and it will lay its head in her lap and fall asleep, and then the hunters will kill it. Bestiary tradition, after the Physiologus

The unicorn is the most *allegorical* animal in the entire bestiary tradition, so it was drawn with a very specific structure: the maiden, the hunters, and the beast, in a fixed three-part arrangement. The animal itself is always drawn as a small, goat-like creature — never a horse — because that's what the description said. The large, horse-shaped unicorn of later tapestries is a different, later rumour, with different motivations and a different visual vocabulary.

06The goose that grows on trees

The hearsay
A tree in Ireland that bears fruit which, when it falls into the water, becomes birds — barnacle geese. Some say it is a shellfish, not a fruit, that becomes a goose. Gerald of Wales, Topographia Hibernica, c. 1188

This is a genuine medieval belief, illustrated as a botanical diagram, not a bestiary illustration. The barnacle goose was a real bird that arrived in Ireland and Britain in winter from an unknown northern breeding ground — so a rumour arose to explain its origins: it grows on trees, or in shells attached to driftwood, before dropping into the sea. Gerald of Wales described it in c. 1188 with a completely straight face, and the illustration shows the goose-and-tree in the style of a botanical plate. The importance of this one is that it isn't drawn from a bestiary tradition at all — it's a scientific explanation of a real biological mystery, rendered as a picture, and it was believed by educated people for centuries.

07The beaver who bites off his own parts

The hearsay
When the beaver is hunted, knowing that its testicles are prized for medicine, it bites them off itself and throws them to the hunter, and so escapes with its life. Bestiary tradition, after Aesop

The beaver is a rare case where the rumour is not about the animal's shape but about its behaviour, and the behaviour is a piece of moral theatre. The beaver's testicles were said to be a medicine, so the beaver, when hunted, castrates itself and throws the parts to the hunter, escaping with its life. The illustration shows this in full detail, because the whole point is the allegory: the beaver is a man who cuts off sinful impulses (and so escapes the devil). The animal itself is drawn as a semi-aquatic dog, because — as usual — the description the artist had was about what the animal does, not what it looks like.

08The hedgehog as a fruit-delivery service

The hearsay
The hedgehog rolls in grapes and carries them skewered on its spines back to its den for its young. Bestiary tradition, after Pliny the Elder

The hedgehog is one of the most functional medieval rumours — the hedgehog is drawn as a fruit-delivery system, with spines like a back-rack for grapes. The behaviour is the point: the hedgehog is an allegory of the wise man who gathers the good things of the world and carries them to safety. And like the beaver, the illustration is driven by what the animal does in the rumour, not by what the animal actually looks like.

09The bonnacon, a weaponised bull

The hearsay
A beast of Paeonia, like a bull, with horns so curled they are useless for fighting — so when it is hunted, it runs away while expelling a trail of dung three acres long, which burns whatever it touches. Bestiary tradition, after Pliny the Elder

The bonnacon is the bestiary tradition's most committed piece of comic rumour. It is drawn exactly like the other quadrupeds — same style, same format, same border — and its absurdity is entirely in the text. The joke is that the bestiary format does not have a register of humour; the bonnacon sits beside the lion and the eagle as if it were a completely normal entry in a completely normal encyclopedia. This is the medieval rumour at its purest: a totally sincere illustration of something that never existed, taken from a text that was itself copying an older text, which was itself copying an older text that had made a joke.

10The yale, with swivelling antlers

The hearsay
A beast of India, the size of a horse, with jaws like a boar and horns that it can swivel in any direction — pointing both forward and back at once, so it can fight on all sides. Bestiary tradition, after Pliny the Elder

The yale was a real heraldic animal — the yale appears on the badges of English royalty, including the Beaufort family — so it survived into early-modern heraldry as an official myth. The interesting thing about it is that it is drawn not as a rumour but as a heraldic emblem: a swivelling-antlered, boar-jawed, horse-bodied creature that embodies defensive versatility. It's one of the few medieval rumour-beasts that made the jump from the bestiary to the coats of arms of actual living families.

11The leucrocuta, hyena gone wrong

The hearsay
An animal of India, born of a hyena and a lioness, with a bone instead of teeth in its mouth, a single continuous row of them, and a voice that imitates human speech — it calls men by name, and when they come, devours them. Bestiary tradition, after Pliny the Elder

The leucrocuta is one of the most linguistically dangerous of the rumour-beasts, because its weapon is a voice that imitates human speech. It calls men by name, and when they come to help, it eats them. This is a rumour about the *danger of trusting what you hear* — which is exactly the anxiety that the whole bestiary tradition is built on. It is a medieval argument, illustrated, that second-hand knowledge can be lethal.

Why it happened

  1. c. 77 AD — Pliny's Natural History
    Pliny the Elder compiled a 37-volume encyclopedia of everything known about the natural world, much of it from older texts he had not verified. This became the single biggest source of medieval animal rumour — the trumpeting elephant, the bonnacon, the yale, the leucrocuta, and dozens more all trace back to Pliny, and all were copied as fact for the next 1,400 years.
  2. c. 200 AD — The Physiologus
    A Greek text that became the template for the entire bestiary tradition. It paired each animal with a Christian allegory, so the illustration's job was not to document the animal but to illustrate the moral. This is why so many medieval animals are drawn in fixed, theatrical poses — the beaver biting itself, the unicorn in the maiden's lap, the whale with a fire on its back.
  3. c. 600 — 636 — Isidore of Seville
    Isidore's Etymologiae explained animals by deriving their names from Latin roots — the crocodile from the yellow colour of the Nile mud, the lion from its name — and his etymologies became descriptions of behaviour. If the name means something, the animal must do that thing. This gave medieval artists a logic for drawing animals they had never seen: the drawing had to agree with the etymology, not with nature.
  4. c. 1200 — The great English bestiaries
    By the thirteenth century, the bestiary had become a luxury book, with large, expensive illustrations — the Aberdeen, Ashmole, Rochester, and Harley bestiaries. The animals in them are drawn with complete artistic confidence, in a consistent house style, with no sense that anything is missing. The artist did not know what an elephant looked like, but he knew exactly how to draw the elephant that Pliny described.
  5. c. 1250 — Matthew Paris draws a real elephant
    An elephant arrived at the Tower of London in 1255, given by Louis IX of France to Henry III. Matthew Paris drew it from life — and it is the most famous animal drawing of the Middle Ages precisely because it shows the collision between a real animal and a rumour-based visual vocabulary. He had the elephant in front of him, and he still drew the trunk as a trumpet, because that's what he had seen in manuscripts.
  6. 1500s onward — The rumour breaks down
    As explorers brought back actual skins, skeletons, and eventually living animals, the bestiary tradition began to collapse. Artist-naturalists like Albrecht Dürer drew a rhinoceros in 1515 from a written description and a sketch by someone who had actually seen one, and it became the definitive European image of a rhino for 200 years — even though the rhino he drew is covered in armour, because the description said its hide was like armour. The last great rumour-beasts, drawn with total sincerity, sit in manuscripts made in the same decade as the first accurate drawings of real ones.

The lesson of medieval animal art is not that artists were stupid. It is that when you draw from a description rather than an observation, the description wins every time — the trumpet-shaped trunk, the goat-like unicorn, the fruit-carrying hedgehog, the bonnacon with its three acres of fire. The rumour becomes the visual vocabulary, and the actual animal has to fight its way back in. That fight, between the word and the thing, is visible in every one of these pictures.
