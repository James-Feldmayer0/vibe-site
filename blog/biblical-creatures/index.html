Angels, Beasts & Monsters: A Visual History of Scripture's Creatures

October 24, 2026 — a chronology, from the oldest depictions to the modern ones

This is a visual history, not a Bible study. For each creature, you get the source verses (with KJV/NIV tabs for reference) and then the art history: a timeline of how artists have imagined that creature, ordered oldest first, through the most notable works, and on to the modern ones. Dates use BC and AD.

The dates tell the real story: some creatures were being painted almost as soon as art survives (the cherubim’s ancestors date to the 9th century BC), while others barely get an image until the printing press. Images come from Wikimedia Commons; where I could not verify a specific image file, the work title links to a Commons search instead of a guess.

I.Seraphim

In the Bible
Isaiah 6:2 ↗
“Above it stood the seraphims: each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly.”
“Above him were seraphim, each with six wings: With two wings they covered their faces, with two they covered their feet, and with two they were flying.”
Isaiah 6:6 ↗
“Then flew one of the seraphims unto me, having a live coal in his hand, which he had taken with the tongs from off the altar:”
“Then one of the seraphim flew to me with a live coal in his hand, which he had taken with tongs from the altar.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Byzantine
    c. 537–562 AD
    Seraphim mosaics of Hagia Sophia
    Byzantine craftsmen — pendentives under the dome, Istanbul
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. The earliest surviving large-scale seraphim. Each has six wings arranged in a cross, two lifted for flight and four wrapped around a hidden body — literal to Isaiah, faceless and burning with colour.

  2. Medieval
    c. 1295–1300 AD
    Stigmatization of St Francis
    Giotto — Louvre, Paris (panel); a fresco version is in the Upper Basilica of St Francis, Assisi
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. Medieval mysticism identified the seraph with Christ crucified, and Giotto paints exactly that: a six-winged seraph bearing the wounds of the crucifixion, raining stigmata down on Francis.

How it changed: from hidden fire to wounded Christ to missing wings. Byzantium kept Isaiah’s literal six-winged faceless seraph; the Middle Ages merged it with the crucified Christ; and in modern fantasy art the seraph has all but vanished — “angels” now get two feathered wings instead of six, which is why Hagia Sophia’s mosaics still startle visitors who expect cupids.

II.Cherubim

In the Bible
Genesis 3:24 ↗
“So he drove out the man; and he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life.”
“After he drove the man out, he placed on the east side of the Garden of Eden cherubim and a flaming sword flashing back and forth to guard the way to the tree of life.”
Exodus 25:20 ↗
“And the cherubims shall stretch forth their wings on high, covering the mercy seat with their wings, and their faces shall look one to another; toward the mercy seat shall the faces of the cherubims be.”
“The cherubim are to have their wings spread upward, overshadowing the cover with them. The cherubim are to face each other, looking toward the cover.”
Ezekiel 1:6 ↗
“And every one had four faces, and every one had four wings.”
“but each of them had four faces and four wings.”
Ezekiel 10:21 ↗
“Every one had four faces apiece, and every one four wings; and the likeness of the hands of a man was under their wings.”
“Each had four faces and four wings, and under their wings was what looked like human hands.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Ancient Near East
    c. 883–859 BC
    Lamassu (winged bull) from Nimrud
    Assyrian, reign of Ashurnasirpal II — British Museum, London
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest by far. Not a cherub, but its ancestor: colossal winged bulls and lions with human heads guarded Assyrian palaces and thrones, and scholars generally trace the Hebrew cherub — a hybrid throne-guardian of God — back to this family of images. Genesis’s cherub is a gatekeeper; this is a gatekeeper in stone.

  2. Renaissance
    c. 1425 AD
    Expulsion from the Garden of Eden
    Masaccio — Brancacci Chapel, Santa Maria del Carmine, Florence
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. The cherub is here only as a tiny red angel waving the flaming sword above Adam and Eve — the terrifying guardian reduced to a punctuation mark at the garden gate.

  3. Post-Byzantine
    16th century AD
    The tetramorph (Cherub of Ezekiel’s vision)
    Metéora monasteries, Greece — in the tradition of Theophanes the Cretan’s workshop
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The tetramorph. While it is painted in the distinctive post-Byzantine monastic tradition of the famous Cretan School of Painting—a style heavily popularized in the Metéora region during this era by masters like Theophanes the Cretan—historical records have not preserved the name of the exact iconographer responsible for this specific piece.

  4. Renaissance
    c. 1512–13 AD
    The putti of the Sistine Madonna
    Raphael — Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister, Dresden (the painting; the two bored winged boys at its foot)
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The turning point. Raphael’s two daydreaming winged boys are classical putti, not cherubim — but English speakers have called them “cherubs” for centuries, and this is the picture that rebranded a four-faced throne-guardian of Ezekiel into a chubby toddler.

How it changed: from monster to mascot. The chain runs Assyrian guardian → four-faced, four-winged bearer of God’s throne → tiny gatekeeper at Eden → winged baby on Valentine’s cards. It is one of the biggest transformations on this page: Ezekiel’s cherubim would be terrifying to meet, and almost nothing of them survives in the popular “cherub”.

III.The four living creatures

In the Bible
Ezekiel 1:10 ↗
“As for the likeness of their faces, they four had the face of a man, and the face of a lion, on the right side: and they four had the face of an ox on the left side; they four also had the face of an eagle.”
“Their faces looked like this: Each of the four had the face of a human being, and on the right side each had the face of a lion, and on the left the face of an ox; each also had the face of an eagle.”
Revelation 4:7 ↗
“And the first beast was like a lion, and the second beast like a calf, and the third beast had a face as a man, and the fourth beast was like a flying eagle.”
“The first living creature was like a lion, the second was like an ox, the third had a face like a man, the fourth was like a flying eagle.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Early Christian
    c. 425 AD
    Dome mosaics of the Mausoleum of Galla Placidia
    Ravenna, Italy — the dome and pendentives of the mausoleum
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. The dome unfolds as a garden-like night sky of golden stars around a jewelled cross — the paradise the creatures guard — while the four pendentives supporting it hold the four creatures as the symbols of the Evangelists — man, lion, ox and eagle — among the earliest surviving set-piece depictions, looking down over the garden for some sixteen centuries.

  2. Medieval
    c. 800 AD
    Evangelist symbols in the Book of Kells
    Insular monks — Trinity College Library, Dublin

    Most notable. The four creatures become inseparable from the Gospels themselves: man for Matthew, lion for Mark, ox for Luke, eagle for John, woven into the most famous decorated book of the Middle Ages.

  3. Gothic
    c. 1145–55 AD
    Royal Portal, Christ in Majesty
    Anonymous sculptors — Chartres Cathedral, France
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    In stone now: Christ in majesty ringed by the four creatures, carved over the cathedral’s main door so every worshipper walked under Revelation’s throne-room.

  4. Renaissance
    c. 1518 AD
    Vision of Ezekiel
    Raphael — Palazzo Pitti, Florence
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Raphael goes back to Ezekiel’s own vision: God borne aloft by the four creatures in full flight, the prophet flung back by the wind of it.

How it changed: from vision to logo. Ezekiel’s composite beings were simplified into four separate creatures by early Christian times, spent a thousand years as the Evangelists’ emblems in mosaic, ink and stone, and finally dissolved into heraldry — the winged lion of St Mark still flies over Venice. In modern art the tetramorph survives almost only in churches.

IV.Michael and the dragon

In the Bible
Revelation 12:7 ↗
“And there was war in heaven: Michael and his angels fought against the dragon; and the dragon fought and his angels,”
“Then war broke out in heaven. Michael and his angels fought against the dragon, and the dragon and his angels fought back.”
Revelation 12:9 ↗
“And the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan, which deceiveth the whole world: he was cast out into the earth, and his angels were cast out with him.”
“The great dragon was hurled down—that ancient serpent called the devil, or Satan, who leads the whole world astray. He was hurled to the earth, and his angels with him.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Byzantine
    6th century AD
    Ivory leaf of the Archangel Michael
    Constantinople — British Museum, London

    The oldest. The earliest monumental Michael is a courtier, not a warrior: robed, holding an orb, no dragon anywhere. The battle of Revelation 12 took centuries to become an image.

  2. Renaissance
    c. 1503–05 AD
    St Michael Vanquishing Satan
    Raphael — Louvre, Paris
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. A slim, elegant young knight in armour pins a writhing demon — the dragon of Revelation rewritten as a personal duel.

  3. Baroque
    c. 1636 AD
    St Michael the Archangel
    Guido Reni — Santa Maria della Concezione dei Cappuccini, Rome
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Reni’s Michael tramples Satan with beautiful contempt — the painting the artist reportedly signed with the face of his enemy, which tells you what the dragon had become: a stand-in for real people.

  4. Modern
    1956–58 AD
    St Michael’s Victory over the Devil
    Jacob Epstein — bronze at the entrance of the new Coventry Cathedral, England
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Modern. Set beside a cathedral built after the 1940 bombing of Coventry, Epstein’s angel is a gaunt, angular figure pinning a horned demon — Revelation’s war in heaven made monumental in a city that had just lived through one.

How it changed: from courtier to knight to monument. Byzantine Michael is a serene official; Renaissance and Baroque Michael is a duellist, his dragon shrinking to a demon underfoot; the modern Michael is carved in bronze for a city rebuilding after war — the creature’s meaning moves with the century.

V.Gabriel

In the Bible
Daniel 9:21 ↗
“Yea, whiles I was speaking in prayer, even the man Gabriel, whom I had seen in the vision at the beginning, being caused to fly swiftly, touched me about the time of the evening oblation.”
“while I was still in prayer, Gabriel, the man I had seen in the earlier vision, came to me in swift flight about the time of the evening sacrifice.”
Luke 1:26 ↗
“And in the sixth month the angel Gabriel was sent from God unto a city of Galilee, named Nazareth,”
“In the sixth month of Elizabeth’s pregnancy, God sent the angel Gabriel to Nazareth, a town in Galilee,”
Luke 1:28 ↗
“And the angel came in unto her, and said, Hail, thou that art highly favoured, the Lord is with thee: blessed art thou among women.”
“The angel went to her and said, ‘Greetings, you who are highly favored! The Lord is with you.’”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Early Christian
    4th century AD
    Annunciation fresco, Catacomb of Priscilla
    Rome — underground burial galleries
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. The earliest surviving Annunciation, painted underground: Gabriel is a small, plain, robed figure addressing Mary — the scene is there, the glory is not.

  2. Renaissance
    c. 1443 AD
    Annunciation, Convent of San Marco
    Fra Angelico — north corridor fresco, Florence
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. Painted on the corridor wall where monks walked to their cells, Gabriel bows with folded wings under a loggia — courtesy, not spectacle.

  3. Renaissance
    c. 1472–76 AD
    Annunciation
    Leonardo da Vinci — Uffizi Gallery, Florence
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Leonardo’s Gabriel kneels in a garden with meticulously observed wings — the angel naturalised into a Renaissance court.

  4. Modern
    1898 AD
    The Annunciation
    Henry Ossawa Tanner — Philadelphia Museum of Art
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Modern. Tanner, an African-American painter working in Paris, strips Gabriel down to a column of swirling luminous form — no face, no wings, no feathers. After 1,500 years of polite courtiers, the angel becomes light again.

How it changed: from messenger to gentleman to light. The earliest Gabriel is a plain robed figure; the Renaissance turns him into a kneeling courtier; and Tanner’s modern Gabriel abandons the body altogether. Each age paints the annunciation with whatever it believes a divine visitation would look like.

VI.The serpent of Eden

In the Bible
Genesis 3:1 ↗
“Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made. And he said unto the woman, Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?”
“Now the serpent was more crafty than any of the wild animals the Lord God had made. He said to the woman, ‘Did God really say, ‘You must not eat from any tree in the garden’?’”
Genesis 3:14 ↗
“And the LORD God said unto the serpent, Because thou hast done this, thou art cursed above all cattle, and above every beast of the field; upon thy belly shalt thou go, and dust shalt thou eat all the days of thy life:”
“So the Lord God said to the serpent, ‘Because you have done this, “Cursed are you above all livestock and all wild animals! You will crawl on your belly and you will eat dust all the days of your life.”’
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Early Christian
    c. 359 AD
    Adam and Eve, Sarcophagus of Junius Bassus
    Rome — now in the Museo Petriano under St Peter’s Basilica
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. Carved on a Roman senator’s marble coffin: the serpent is a small snake coiled up beside the trees, present and blameable but no monster at all.

  2. Northern Renaissance
    c. 1470 AD
    The Fall of Man
    Hugo van der Goes — Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The serpent has grown a hairy, half-human body — a creature between beast and demon, whispering at Eve’s ear.

  3. Renaissance
    1508–12 AD
    Original Sin and Expulsion, Sistine Chapel ceiling
    Michelangelo — Vatican
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. The famous woman-torsoed serpent coiled around the tree, handing the fruit down — the single most influential image of the creature, even though the Bible says nothing of the kind.

  4. Victorian
    1866 AD
    Illustrations to Milton’s Paradise Lost
    Gustave Doré — engraved for the London edition

    Modern. Doré shows Satan entering and possessing the serpent before the Fall — the snake becomes a puppet for the devil, the reading most Christians absorb even today.

How it changed: from snake to monster and back to snake. Early art keeps the Bible’s plain reptile; the Middle Ages and Renaissance give it human features to make temptation personal (culminating in Michelangelo’s serpent-woman); Doré’s Victorian Satan merges the two creatures entirely; and modern depictions usually settle for a plain snake, occasionally with a knowing face.

VII.The fiery serpents

In the Bible
Numbers 21:6 ↗
“And the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and much people of Israel died.”
“Then the Lord sent venomous snakes among them; they bit the people and many Israelites died.”
Numbers 21:8 ↗
“And the LORD said unto Moses, Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live.”
“The Lord said to Moses, ‘Make a snake and put it up on a pole; anyone who is bitten can look at it and live.’”
Numbers 21:9 ↗
“And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived.”
“So Moses made a bronze snake and put it up on a pole. Then when anyone was bitten by a snake and looked at the bronze snake, they lived.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    before 1500 AD

    No ancient depiction survives securely identified. Unlike the Eden serpent, this plague-and-cure scene apparently enters art with the Renaissance — the creatures themselves (vipers, and Moses’ brass effigy) have almost no surviving old depictions.

  2. Renaissance
    1511 AD
    The Brazen Serpent
    Michelangelo — pendentive of the Sistine Chapel ceiling, Vatican
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. The brass serpent writhes on its pole above a knot of bodies — the plague rendered as pure mass and strain on a curving ceiling vault.

  3. Renaissance
    c. 1575–76 AD
    The Brazen Serpent
    Tintoretto — Scuola Grande di San Rocco, Venice
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Tintoretto spins the whole scene around the pole in violent foreshortening — serpents underfoot everywhere, survivors straining to look up.

  4. Baroque
    c. 1635–40 AD
    The Brazen Serpent
    Peter Paul Rubens — National Gallery, London
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Modern (Baroque). Rubens crowds the canvas with the dying and the saved, Moses raising the brass serpent at the centre like a standard in battle.

How it changed: from an emblem to an emergency. The Bible’s image is almost medical — one serpent on a pole, look and live — and Renaissance and Baroque artists inflated it into catastrophes of writhing bodies. The pole-and-serpent itself outlived them all: it lives on every medical insignia that echoes the serpent-on-a-staff.

VIII.Leviathan

In the Bible
Job 41:1 ↗
“Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook? or his tongue with a cord which thou lettest down?”
“Can you pull in Leviathan with a fishhook or tie down its tongue with a rope?”
Psalm 74:14 ↗
“Thou brakest the heads of leviathan in pieces, and gavest him to be meat to the people inhabiting the wilderness.”
“It was you who crushed the heads of Leviathan and gave it as food to the creatures of the desert.”
Isaiah 27:1 ↗
“In that day the LORD with his sore and great and strong sword shall punish leviathan the piercing serpent, even leviathan that crooked serpent; and he shall slay the dragon that is in the sea.”
“In that day, the Lord will punish with his sword—his fierce, great and powerful sword—Leviathan the gliding serpent, Leviathan the coiling serpent; he will slay the monster of the sea.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    before 1800 AD

    Leviathan is strangely rare in old art. No surviving ancient or medieval depiction can be confidently pointed to as “the” Leviathan — a sea chaos-monster appears in general contexts, but the creature from Job gets almost no images for most of history. Artists finally reach it when printed illustration makes Job a subject.

  2. Romantic
    1825–26 AD
    Behemoth and Leviathan (plate 15 of the Book of Job series)
    William Blake — engraved and printed in London

    Most notable. God’s tour of the two untameable beasts: Leviathan as a coiling dragon of the deep, drawn in Blake’s sinuous line and printed alongside Job’s text.

  3. Victorian
    1865 AD
    The Destruction of Leviathan
    Gustave Doré — wood engraving for La Sainte Bible, Tours
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Modern. Isaiah’s prophecy at last illustrated: a radiant sword of God splits a mountainous sea-dragon, the engraving that fixed in the popular mind what Leviathan “looks like”.

How it changed: from word to whale. For most of history Leviathan lived only in text; Blake gave it a body made of curves, Doré gave it a spectacular death, and modern culture took the name for anything enormous in the sea — whales, submarines, and the occasional film monster.

IX.Behemoth

In the Bible
Job 40:15 ↗
“Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee; he eateth grass as an ox.”
“Look at Behemoth, which I made along with you and which feeds on grass like an ox.”
Job 40:17 ↗
“He moveth his tail like a cedar: the sinews of his stones are wrapped together.”
“Its tail sways like a cedar; the sinews of its thighs are close-knit.”
Job 40:23 ↗
“Behold, he drinketh up a river, and hasteth not: he trusteth that he can draw up Jordan into his mouth.”
“A raging river does not alarm it; it is secure, though the Jordan should surge against its mouth.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    before 1825 AD

    Like Leviathan, almost undepicted for most of history. Medieval readers thought of an elephant or an ox, but no surviving ancient depiction of “Behemoth” as such is securely identified; the creature’s visual history effectively begins in the 1820s.

  2. Romantic
    1825–26 AD
    Behemoth and Leviathan (plate 15 of the Book of Job series)
    William Blake — engraved and printed in London
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable and effectively the oldest. Blake paints Behemoth as an elephantine grass-eater crouched among reeds “under the shady trees”, a river rolling past — the first famous picture of the creature, paired with Leviathan in the deep.

  3. Modern
    20th–21st century AD
    Behemoth as a sauropod dinosaur
    Young-earth creationist illustration — creation museums and tracts

    Modern. Because Job 40:17 says “he moveth his tail like a cedar”, a whole genre of modern artwork paints Behemoth as a Brachiosaurus-like sauropod grazing beside a river — an interpretive leap that would have baffled Blake, and the clearest example on this page of art serving a modern reading of the text.

How it changed: from ox to elephant to dinosaur. Tradition guessed ox or elephant; Blake drew the elephant; and the 20th century, armed with dinosaurs, redraws him as a sauropod. No creature on this page shows more clearly how the artist’s world decides what the verse must mean.

X.The great red dragon

In the Bible
Revelation 12:3 ↗
“And there appeared another wonder in heaven; and behold a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns, and seven crowns upon his heads.”
“Then another sign appeared in heaven: an enormous red dragon with seven heads and ten horns and seven crowns on its heads.”
Revelation 12:4 ↗
“And his tail drew the third part of the stars of heaven, and did cast them to the earth: and the dragon stood before the woman which was ready to be delivered, for to devour her child as soon as it was born.”
“Its tail swept a third of the stars out of the sky and flung them to the earth. The dragon stood in front of the woman who was about to give birth, so that it might devour her child the moment he was born.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Medieval
    10th–11th century AD
    Beatus manuscripts — the Dragon cast into hell
    Monastic scriptoria of Spain — e.g. the Saint-Sever Beatus, c. 1050
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. The Beatus commentary manuscripts of Spain carry the oldest surviving Apocalypse illustrations, from the 10th century onward: here the dragon is a brightly coloured, multi-headed beast painted flat, its heads a decorative ring.

  2. Medieval
    c. 1377–82 AD
    The Apocalypse Tapestry
    Jean de Bruges (designer), Nicolas Bataille (weaver) — Château d’Angers, France

    Most notable (medieval). A 100-metre woven Apocalypse, the largest medieval tapestry cycle: the red dragon appears scene after scene in full courtly colour.

  3. Renaissance
    1498 AD
    The Dragon with the Seven Heads (Apocalypse woodcuts)
    Albrecht Dürer — woodcut, Nuremberg
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Dürer’s dragon becomes a proper Western dragon — winged, scaly, coiling through the sky in woodcut line — and the printed Apocalypse spread that image across Europe.

  4. Romantic
    c. 1805–10 AD
    The Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed with the Sun
    William Blake — Brooklyn Museum, New York (watercolour series)
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Modern (Romantic). Blake keeps every number in Revelation — seven heads, ten horns, seven crowns — and stretches the dragon to the top of the page like a bat-winged skyscraper over the tiny woman. It is the most famous single depiction of any creature on this list.

How it changed: from ornament to anatomy. The Beatus dragon is heads and colour, Dürer’s is a scaly animal with correct musculature, Blake’s is a sublime architectural monster — and modern fantasy dragons (from Smaug to the red dragon on the flag of Wales) descend from Dürer and Blake’s version far more than from the flat beasts of the manuscripts.

XI.The beast from the sea

In the Bible
Revelation 13:1 ↗
“And I stood upon the sand of the sea, and saw a beast rise up out of the sea, having seven heads and ten horns, and upon his horns ten crowns, and upon his heads the name of blasphemy.”
“The dragon stood on the shore of the sea. And I saw a beast coming out of the sea. It had ten horns and seven heads, with ten crowns on its horns, and on each head a blasphemous name.”
Revelation 13:2 ↗
“And the beast which I saw was like unto a leopard, and his feet were as the feet of a bear, and his mouth as the mouth of a lion: and the dragon gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority.”
“The beast I saw resembled a leopard, but had feet like those of a bear and a mouth like that of a lion. The dragon gave the beast his power and his throne and great authority.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Medieval
    c. 970 AD
    Beatus of Valladolid, folio 135r — the Beast from the sea
    Spanish monastic scriptorium — Biblioteca Universitaria, Valladolid
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. Among the earliest surviving pictures of Revelation 13 specifically: the beast stands half-risen from the sea, seven heads spread like a fan, painted in manuscript colour a thousand years ago.

  2. Medieval
    c. 1377–82 AD
    The Apocalypse Tapestry
    Jean de Bruges (designer), Nicolas Bataille (weaver) — Château d’Angers, France

    Most notable (medieval). The woven beast at Angers wears its ten crowns like a courtly king — medieval viewers read it as empire and persecuting power, which is exactly the political allegory it was.

  3. Renaissance
    1498 AD
    Dürer’s Apocalypse woodcuts
    Albrecht Dürer — Nuremberg

    Dürer gives the composite leopard-bear-lion animal plausible anatomy and a printed audience; from his woodcuts the beast walks into centuries of political cartoons.

How it changed: from puzzle-creature to political cartoon. Early artists assembled the parts faithfully (leopard body, bear feet, lion mouth, seven heads); the Middle Ages dressed it in crowns; and since Dürer the beast has been endlessly re-cast as whichever empire each artist feared most — making it the most politically reused creature in the Bible.

XII.The locusts of the abyss

In the Bible
Revelation 9:7 ↗
“And the shapes of the locusts were like unto horses prepared unto battle; and on their heads were as it were crowns like gold, and their faces were as the faces of men.”
“The locusts looked like horses prepared for battle. On their heads they wore something like crowns of gold, and their faces resembled human faces.”
Revelation 9:10 ↗
“And they had tails like unto scorpions, and there were stings in their tails: and their power was to hurt men five months.”
“They had tails with stingers, like scorpions, and in their tails they had power to torment people for five months.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Medieval
    10th century AD onward
    Beatus manuscripts
    Spanish monastic scriptoria — e.g. Girona and Saint-Sever Beatus manuscripts

    The oldest. The Beatus Apocalypse cycle includes the sounding of the fifth trumpet: man-faced, horse-bodied, scorpion-tailed locusts crawling out of the smoke — the strangest parts of Revelation 9 taken literally in paint.

  2. Medieval
    c. 1377–82 AD
    The Apocalypse Tapestry
    Jean de Bruges (designer), Nicolas Bataille (weaver) — Château d’Angers, France

    Most notable (medieval). The locusts woven into the Angers cycle keep their human faces and scorpion tails — courtly, elegant, and horrifying all at once.

  3. Renaissance
    1498 AD
    The Opening of the Fifth and Sixth Trumpets (Apocalypse woodcuts)
    Albrecht Dürer — woodcut, Nuremberg

    Dürer crowds the woodcut with armoured horse-locusts raining down on fleeing people — the printed image that most modern readers subconsciously picture.

How it changed: from hybrid to horror-movie swarm. The medieval literal hybrids (man faces, horse bodies, scorpion tails) gave way in modern illustration to something closer to reality’s nightmare: vast swarming clouds. The old hybrids survive mainly in manuscripts and metal albums; the fear is unchanged.

XIII.The Nephilim

In the Bible
Genesis 6:4 ↗
“There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown.”
“The Nephilim were on the earth in those days—and also afterward—when the sons of God went to the daughters of humans and had children by them. They were the heroes of old, men of renown.”
Numbers 13:33 ↗
“And there we saw the giants, the sons of Anak, which come of the giants: and we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight.”
“We saw the Nephilim there (the descendants of Anak come from the Nephilim). We seemed like grasshoppers in our own eyes, and we looked the same to them.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    before 1800 AD

    I can’t confidently name a major canonical artwork depicting the Nephilim specifically, so I won’t list one. Giant-descended peoples appear incidentally in old art, but the Nephilim of Genesis 6 as a subject is barely painted before modernity.

  2. Modern
    20th–21st century AD
    Nephilim in modern fantasy art
    Book covers, tabletop games, and film concept art

    Modern. The Nephilim finally got pictures — in fiction. Modern fantasy renders them as towering humanoids, often half-angel, an image that comes from popular books and games rather than any tradition of biblical illustration.

How it changed: from a verse to a franchise. For most of history the Nephilim were left to commentary; the modern era, hungry for giants, gave them a consistent pop-culture body. This is a creature whose depictions are essentially invented after 1900.

XIV.Satan

In the Bible
Job 1:6 ↗
“Now there was a day when the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and Satan came also among them.”
“One day the angels came to present themselves before the Lord, and Satan also came with them.”
Zechariah 3:1 ↗
“And he shewed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to resist him.”
“Then he showed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the Lord, and Satan standing at his right side to accuse him.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    before 1800 AD

    Satan’s oldest depictions are hard to name securely. Early art shows the devil tempting Christ, but no single “Job’s Satan in the heavenly court” image survives from antiquity that I can confidently cite. In the Bible itself he is a prosecuting accuser among the “sons of God” — not the horned creature of later art.

  2. Romantic
    1825–26 AD
    Satan before the Throne of God (plate 2 of the Book of Job series)
    William Blake — engraved and printed in London
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. Blake restores Job’s original: Satan as a beautiful, upright figure presenting himself before God’s throne — no horns, no bat wings, no red. The accuser is back on the celestial payroll.

  3. Romantic
    1827 AD
    Illustrations to Milton’s Paradise Lost
    John Martin — mezzotints published in London

    Martin’s Satan broods over vast apocalyptic architecture — the Miltonic fallen angel as sublime landscape, the image that dominates 19th-century imagination of the devil.

  4. Victorian
    1866 AD
    Illustrations to Milton’s Paradise Lost
    Gustave Doré — engraved for the London edition

    Modern. Doré’s dark, feathered, dramatic Satan completes the Miltonic tradition — a tragic antihero, which is a long way from Zechariah’s courtroom adversary, and the ancestor of most modern “devil” imagery.

How it changed: from accuser to fallen angel to cartoon devil. The Bible’s Satan is an official in God’s court; Milton and Blake made him a tragic hero; Doré made him operatic; and popular culture flattened him into the horned red figure — a shape the Bible never describes. Blake’s plate 2 is the rare major artwork that paints the actual Job passage.

XV.Balaam’s talking donkey

In the Bible
Numbers 22:28 ↗
“And the LORD opened the mouth of the ass, and she said unto Balaam, What have I done unto thee, that thou hast smitten me these three times?”
“Then the Lord opened the donkey’s mouth, and it said to Balaam, ‘What have I done to you to make you beat me these three times?’”
Numbers 22:30 ↗
“And the ass said unto Balaam, Am not I thine ass, upon which thou hast ridden ever since I was thine unto this day? was I ever wont to do so unto thee? And he said, Nay.”
“The donkey said to Balaam, ‘Am I not your own donkey, which you have always ridden, to this day? Have I been in the habit of doing this to you?’ ‘No,’ he said.”
In art — oldest to newest
  1. Early Jewish
    c. 244 AD
    Wall paintings of the synagogue at Dura-Europos
    Dura-Europos, Syria — panels now conserved at Yale University Art Gallery
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. The 3rd-century synagogue wall paintings include panels of Balaam, his donkey and the blocking angel — among the earliest surviving narrative paintings of any Hebrew Bible story, anywhere.

  2. Baroque
    1626 AD
    Balaam and the Ass
    Rembrandt — Musée Cognacq-Jay, Paris
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. Rembrandt, aged about 20, makes the panic the subject: the donkey cringes under the raised angel’s sword while Balaam thrashes, the comic terror of Numbers 22 in one early canvas.

How it changed: from a wall panel to a comic scene. At Dura-Europos the donkey is a small element in a big prophetic composition; Rembrandt builds the whole picture around the animal’s fright; and modern depictions are rare — the scene is simply too odd for most art after the 17th century.

XVI.The unicorn (re’em)

In the Bible
Numbers 23:22 ↗
“God brought them out of Egypt; he hath as it were the strength of an unicorn.”
“God brought them out of Egypt; they have the strength of a wild ox.”
Job 39:9 ↗
“Will the unicorn be willing to serve thee, or abide by thy crib?”
“Will the wild ox consent to serve you? Will it stay by your manger at night?”
Job 39:10 ↗
“Canst thou bind the unicorn with his band in the furrow? or will he harrow the valleys after thee?”
“Can you hold it to the furrow with a harness? Will it till the valleys behind you?”
Psalm 92:10 ↗
“But my horn shalt thou exalt like the horn of an unicorn: I shall be anointed with fresh oil.”
“You have exalted my horn like that of a wild ox; fine oils have been poured on me.”

A note on translation: the Hebrew word here is re’em, which most modern Bibles render “wild ox”. The “unicorn” is a KJV rendering, so this creature is half translation and half animal.

In art — oldest to newest
  1. Medieval
    c. 1200 AD
    The unicorn in the Aberdeen Bestiary
    English or French workshop — folio 15r, now University of Aberdeen
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    The oldest. Medieval bestiaries turn the KJV’s unicorn (and the Latin monoceros behind it) into a fierce one-horned beast that can only be tamed by a maiden — an allegory of Christ and the Virgin, painted in the margins of learned books.

  2. Medieval
    c. 1495–1505 AD
    The Unicorn Tapestries — “The Unicorn in Captivity”
    South Netherlandish workshops — The Met Cloisters, New York
    Image via Wikimedia Commons

    Most notable. The chained unicorn in the flower-filled pen is the last panel of the famous tapestry cycle — an allegory of the tamed and devoted creature, and the picture that made the unicorn iconic.

How it changed: from a strong ox to a tamed myth to a mascot. Job’s untameable one-horned beast became, through medieval allegory, a captured Christ-symbol, then a heraldic animal, and finally the pink rainbow mascot of children’s products. Virtually none of the modern unicorn’s meaning survives from the verses that put the word in the KJV.

XVII.The satyrs (se’irim)

In the Bible
Leviticus 17:7 ↗
“And they shall no more offer their sacrifices unto devils, after whom they have gone a whoring. This shall be a statute for ever unto them throughout their generations.”
“They must no longer offer any of their sacrifices to the goat idols to whom they prostitute themselves. This is to be a lasting ordinance for them and for the generations to come.”
Isaiah 13:21 ↗
“But wild beasts of the desert shall lie there; and their houses shall be full of doleful creatures; and owls shall dwell there, and satyrs shall dance there.”
“But desert creatures will lie there, jackals will fill her houses; there the owls will dwell, and there the wild goats will leap about.”
Isaiah 34:14 ↗
“The wild beasts of the desert shall also meet with the wild beasts of the island, and the satyr shall cry to his fellow; the screech owl also shall rest there, and find for herself a place of rest.”
“Desert creatures will meet with hyenas, and wild goats will bleat to each other; there the night creatures will also lie down and find for themselves places of rest.”

A note on translation: the Hebrew word here is se’irim — “hairy ones”, goat-demons in most modern translations. The KJV’s “satyrs” borrows a creature from Greek myth that the Hebrew text likely never meant.

In art — oldest to newest
  1. Antiquity
    —

    I can’t confidently name a major canonical artwork depicting the biblical satyrs specifically, so I won’t list one rather than guess. Greek satyrs fill ancient art, but that is a different creature wearing the KJV’s borrowed name.

How it changed: from goat-demon to translation accident. Whatever the se’irim were (goat-demons, or shaggy wild goats), they were essentially never depicted as such — the KJV’s Greek label sent English readers imagining Pan’s followers in a ruined Babylon. It is the clearest case on this page of a creature created by translation rather than by art.

Gaps and cautions

Honest gaps. Where I could not confidently name a genuine depiction (the Nephilim and the satyrs; ancient depictions of Leviathan, Behemoth and Satan), I said so instead of inventing one.

Dates. Several are approximate (“c.”), dating conventions for manuscripts and excavated walls vary, and museum holdings change — the works get loaned and moved, so treat locations as current-as-of-writing.

Translation. The unicorn and the satyrs are KJV renderings; most modern Bibles read re’em as “wild ox” and se’irim as “goat-demons” — two of the creatures above depend on the translation you quote.

Images. Every embedded image points at a Wikimedia Commons file page that I verified returns HTTP 200. Where no verified file exists, the work title links to a Commons search rather than a broken guess.

Scope. Jonah’s great fish is a natural animal, and the four horsemen and Gog and Magog are not creatures in the same sense — but I can add them.
