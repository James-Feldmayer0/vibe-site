Nobody tells you when the cool stuff is on

October 23, 2026 — a Sydney dashboard and the pitch behind it

The problem isn't a lack of events

Every city is full of things to do. That's not the problem. The problem is that discovery is scattered, timing is fuzzy, and knowing ≠ going. The laneway festival you'd have loved ended last weekend; the farmers market you keep meaning to hit is on tomorrow morning and you find out at 6pm the night before. Nobody tells you when the cool stuff is on — not in one place, not in time, not in a format you can act on.

Facebook Events: a great database behind a cringe app

Here's the awkward truth: Facebook Events is still the biggest events database in the world, and almost everything worth doing is listed there. But the venue is cringe — you have to be on the platform, the interface is a maze, and half the good events are buried in groups or word-of-mouth shares.

The cool move is obviously to quietly extract the events and leave the platform behind. Being honest about it: direct scraping is a ToS and technical nightmare, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But the spirit of it — extract the signal — is very doable through legitimate routes: official public APIs, event aggregators, and council feeds. The data wants to be a plain list.

The fix: one boring page

So the pitch is this: a single boring page that answers one question — what's on, soon, near me? That's it. The artifact above is the prototype: one dashboard, filtered to this weekend, with culture and food tags, stars for shortlisting, and a Maps link on every card. No feed, no notifications, no social layer — just a list you can scan in thirty seconds and act on.

Boring is the feature. Every app in this space dies by trying to become a social network. A list doesn't need network effects to be useful.

The group-chat rules that make it work

  • Never drop an event without an end date and a link. "Some market thing on Sunday" is not an event; it's a rumour. End date so nobody shows up after it's packed up, link so nobody has to search for it.
  • Star-vote to pick plans. Everyone drops a ⭐ on what they'd actually go to, and the highest score wins. No more three-day "should we do something?" threads.
  • Subscribe to the sources, not the platforms. The dashboard is just a renderer — the real work is the intake list.

A field guide of sources that still work

  • Council pages. Genuinely underrated — local governments publish clean, dated event listings, and nobody reads them.
  • Venue mailing lists. The old way is still the best way. Sign up and let the good stuff arrive on its own schedule.
  • Regulars and fixtures. Markets, screenings, trivia nights — recurring events don't need discovery, they need a calendar entry made once.
  • The friend who is a human RSS feed. Every group has one. They know what's on before the posters go up. Buy them dinner occasionally.

That's the whole idea. Discovery doesn't need another app — it needs one boring page, a couple of group-chat rules, and a habit of putting things in the calendar while they still exist.
